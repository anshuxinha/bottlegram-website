Disaster management

Disaster management

8 October 2026. Dr. Anshuman Sinha.

A disaster, in the public health sense, is a disruption that exceeds what the affected community can cope with using its own resources. An accident that the local hospital can absorb is an emergency. The same event becomes a disaster when the beds, the water, or the staff run out. Community Medicine answers are about that gap, not about the clinical management of one fracture.

What is the disaster management cycle?

  • Prevention and mitigation. Work done before the event so that the same hazard causes less harm. A flood embankment, a building code, a stock of oral rehydration in a cyclone district.
  • Preparedness. Plans, drills, and stores, while the hazard has not yet arrived. This is where a hospital writes its mass-casualty plan.
  • Response. The hours and days after impact: search, rescue, triage, water, shelter, and the first surveillance for outbreaks.
  • Recovery. Restoring services and, where possible, building them back so the next event is smaller. Recovery overlaps with mitigation. They are not the same week.

Students often write only response. A long answer that never mentions preparedness loses the marks that were sitting on the cycle.

How does triage sort people?

In a mass-casualty incident the usual field colours are:

  • Red. Immediate. The person will die or deteriorate without an intervention that can still be given here.
  • Yellow. Delayed. Serious, and stable enough to wait.
  • Green. Minimal. Walking wounded.
  • Black. Expectant or dead. With the resources on the ground, survival is not expected.

Triage is repeated. A yellow patient becomes red when the bleeding that looked controlled does not stay controlled. The colour is a decision about resources, not a diagnosis.

What does public health do after the rescue?

Water, sanitation, shelter, food, and vaccination where an outbreak is a real risk. Surveillance looks for clusters of diarrhoea, measles, and respiratory infection, because those are what crowded camps produce. Dead bodies, in most natural disasters, are not the epidemic risk that folklore claims. The living, crowded and without water, are.

India's institutional frame is the Disaster Management Act, 2005: a national authority, state authorities, and district authorities. The international frame students are asked to name is the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction. You do not need the article numbers. You need the idea that the work is supposed to happen before the flood, at district level, and not only in a relief camp.
