Family planning calculation

Pearl index, with a worked example

8 October 2026. Dr. Anshuman Sinha.

What is the Pearl index?

The Pearl index is the number of accidental pregnancies per 100 woman-years of exposure. It is one of the two usual ways to describe how often a contraceptive method fails. The other is life-table analysis.

Failure rate per 100 woman-years = (total accidental pregnancies ÷ total months of exposure) × 1,200.

  • The numerator includes every known conception, whatever happened afterwards: a live birth, a stillbirth, an abortion, or a pregnancy that has not ended yet.
  • The denominator is the months people were actually at risk. The usual deduction is 10 months for each full-term pregnancy and 4 months for each abortion.
  • 1,200 is the number of months in 100 years. It turns a monthly count into a rate per 100 woman-years.
  • A study with fewer than about 600 months of exposure is usually too small for a firm failure rate.

If the question already gives woman-years, do not multiply by 1,200. The rate is then (pregnancies ÷ woman-years) × 100.

A worked example

Suppose a clinic records 4 accidental pregnancies across 800 woman-months. These figures are for the arithmetic. They are not the published failure rate of any method.

Pearl index = (4 ÷ 800) × 1,200 = 6.

That is 6 pregnancies per 100 woman-years. The same 4 pregnancies across 2,400 woman-months give (4 ÷ 2,400) × 1,200 = 2. The method did not become safer. The follow-up got longer, so the denominator grew.

Why does a longer study look better?

The Pearl index treats the failure rate as if it stayed the same from the first month to the last. It does not, for two reasons.

  • The most fertile couples conceive early and then leave the denominator. The people still under observation later are, on average, less fertile, so the same method looks better the longer the survivors are watched.
  • People get better at using a method. Early mistakes raise the first months. A three-year index mixes those months with later, more skilled use, and reports one number.

A Pearl index from a six-month study and a Pearl index from a five-year study are not interchangeable. When you quote one, say how long the observation lasted.

What does life-table analysis add?

Life-table analysis gives the cumulative chance of pregnancy by a fixed time, such as 1 year, 3 years, or 5 years. Two methods can then be compared at the same cut-off. When a paper gives both, the life-table rate at one year is the fairer comparison, and the Pearl index is the older summary.
