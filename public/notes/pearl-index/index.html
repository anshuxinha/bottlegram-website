Family planning calculation

Pearl index, with a worked example

Updated 8 October 2026. Written by Dr. Anshuman Sinha. The numbers below are a practice set, not a published failure rate of any method.

The Pearl index is a measure of how often a contraceptive method fails. It is the number of accidental pregnancies per 100 woman-years of use. MBBS answers need the formula and one limitation. MD answers need the limitation spelled out, because that is why two published Pearl indexes often cannot be compared.

The formula

Pearl index = (accidental pregnancies ÷ months of exposure) × 1,200.

The 1,200 is 12 months times 100 women. Multiplying by 1,200 turns a monthly rate into a rate per 100 woman-years. Accidental pregnancies means every unwanted pregnancy in the study, whatever happened afterwards: a live birth, an abortion, a stillbirth, or a pregnancy still continuing. You do not drop a pregnancy because it ended.

Months of exposure are the months people were actually at risk of a method failure. A full-term pregnancy is not months of contraceptive exposure. Neither is the time after someone stops the method. A study that never says how it counted those months has not given you a Pearl index. You have been given a slogan.

Work the numbers

A clinic follows a method and records 4 accidental pregnancies across 800 woman-months of exposure.

Pearl index = (4 ÷ 800) × 1,200 = 0.005 × 1,200 = 6.

Read that as 6 pregnancies per 100 woman-years. Check the arithmetic before you trust a remembered "typical" number from a senior's notes. If the question gives pregnancies and woman-years instead of months, do not multiply by 1,200. A rate per 100 woman-years is already (pregnancies ÷ woman-years) × 100. Mixing the two denominators is the usual calculation error.

Second check. Same 4 pregnancies, but the follow-up was 2,400 woman-months because the study ran longer.

Pearl index = (4 ÷ 2,400) × 1,200 = 2.

The method did not become three times safer. The index fell because the denominator grew. That is the drawback.

Why a longer study looks better

The Pearl index treats the failure rate as if it were the same in month 1 and month 36. It is not, for two reasons.

  • The most fertile couples conceive early and then leave the denominator. The couples still in the study later are, on average, less fertile. The same method looks better the longer you watch the survivors.
  • People get better at using a method. Early mistakes inflate the first months. A three-year Pearl index mixes clumsy first months with skilled later months and reports one number.

So a Pearl index from a six-month study and a Pearl index from a five-year study are not the same kind of fact. Quoting the smaller one as "the" failure rate of the method is how advertisements work, and how exam answers lose marks.

What to say instead when the question allows it

Life-table analysis reports the cumulative chance of pregnancy by a fixed time: at 1 year, at 3 years, at 5 years. Two methods can then be compared at the same cut-off. If a paper gives both, quote the life-table rate at one year and mention the Pearl index only as the older summary. If a paper gives only a Pearl index, say the duration of observation before you compare it with anything else.
