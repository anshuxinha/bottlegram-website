Epidemiology calculation

Sensitivity, specificity, and predictive value

8 October 2026. Dr. Anshuman Sinha. The table below is a teaching example.

What do sensitivity and specificity mean?

Sensitivity is the ability of a test to pick up people who have the disease. It is the true positives divided by everyone with the disease. Specificity is the ability of the test to leave healthy people alone. It is the true negatives divided by everyone without the disease. Those two describe the test.

Predictive values describe a result in a particular population. The positive predictive value is the chance that a person with a positive test actually has the disease. The negative predictive value is the chance that a person with a negative test does not. Prevalence changes the predictive values. It does not change sensitivity or specificity.

A 2-by-2 table

One thousand people are tested. One hundred of them have the disease. The test calls 90 of those 100 positive, and it calls 80 healthy people positive.

How do you calculate each one?

  • Sensitivity = 90 ÷ 100 = 90%. Of the people with the disease, 90% are caught.
  • Specificity = 820 ÷ 900 = 91.1%. Of the healthy people, about 91% are correctly called negative. Show the fraction before the rounded percent.
  • Positive predictive value = 90 ÷ 170 = 52.9%. In this population a positive result is right about half the time.
  • Negative predictive value = 820 ÷ 830 = 98.8%.
  • Prevalence = 100 ÷ 1,000 = 10%.

Sensitivity does not use the false positives, and specificity does not use the false negatives. If a formula reaches into the other column, the definition has been swapped.

Why does the same test look worse when the disease is rare?

Keep sensitivity at 90% and specificity at 91.1%, and suppose only 10 of 1,000 people have the disease.

  • True positives are about 9. False negatives are about 1.
  • Healthy people = 990. False positives = 80 ÷ 900 × 990 = 88. True negatives = 902.
  • Positive predictive value = 9 ÷ 97, about 9.3%.

The test is unchanged. Most positive results are now healthy people, because the disease is uncommon. A test that looked useful in a hospital clinic can fill a camp with false alarms. Screening is still worth doing when the positives can be confirmed.

Where does screening sit among the levels of prevention?

Screening is secondary prevention: the disease has started, and the aim is to find it early. That placement is on the concept of health and disease page. A test used after symptoms have started is a diagnostic test, even when the arithmetic is the same.
